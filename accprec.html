precision accelerometer

with low noise and small drift,

3 axes, USB or RS-485 interfaces, powered via USB,

software- switchable acceleration ranges ±2g/±4g/±8g

The new precision MEMS accelerometer with low noise and small drift is designed for measuring small accelerations and features a wide dynamic range. The high sensitivity of the accelerometer lets you use it for seismic measurements as well.

Precision accelerometers with an RS-485 interface

 
The precision RS-485 accelerometer connects to a computer's USB port, and it can transmit data over a cable several dozen meters long.
The kit includes a precision accelerometer board and an RS-485<>USB adapter board.
The precision accelerometer is powered via USB from the computer.
The dimensions of the precision accelerometer board are 29x29mm; it can be trimmed to a circle 29mm in diameter to fit into the enclosure shown in the following photo:
           

After the cable is soldered, the RS-485<>USB adapter can be placed inside heat-shrink tubing, producing a sturdy, rigid enclosure.

Parameters and features of a precision digital accelerometer with low noise. 

The precision accelerometer with low noise level (referred to below as the 8g accelerometer) uses a 20-bit ADC,
In previous accelerometers, working in the most sensitive ±2g range used 10 bits of the ADC.

The number of acceleration ranges of the 8g accelerometer has been reduced to: ±2g/±4g/±8g
The accelerometer uses three low-pass filters:
- a fixed analog filter with a cutoff frequency of 1.5kHz, suppressing the 4kHz sampling rate,
- a fixed digital filter,
- a digital filter whose cutoff frequency depends on the sampling rate.

Sampling rate Fs, Hz                  4000   2000   1000   500   250    125      62.5       31.25     15.625    7.8125       3.90625
Low-pass filter cutoff frequency, Hz        1000     500     250   125   62.5   31.25   15.625   7.8125   3.90625  1.953125   0.9765625

To remove the DC component or suppress a slowly varying acceleration, a high-pass filter is provided; it can be left disabled - in that case the acceleration will contain a DC component.
When the high-pass filter is enabled, its cutoff frequency is defined as a portion of the sampling rate Fs and can be selected from the series:
High-pass filter cutoff frequency:  0.00247*Fs   0.00062084*Fs   0.00015545*Fs   0.00003862*Fs   0.00000954*Fs   0.000002380000*Fs  or  0 (filter disabled)

MEMS accelerometers run off an internal clock oscillator, whose accuracy and stability aren't high. For lengthy recordings tied to real time, clocking from an external source of a precise and stable reference frequency is required.
The 8g accelerometer can operate in a mode clocked by a signal from an external oscillator.

Testing results for the precision accelerometer. 

Measurements of small accelerations on a swinging calibrator.

A swinging (pendulum) calibrator is a platform suspended on four hangers, each 60cm long. As it swings, the platform stays horizontal. The main acceleration is generated along the direction of swing; there is also acceleration along the vertical axis.
The acceleration was measured with the precision RS-485 accelerometer, connected using a cable of thin, flexible wires to keep damping of the platform's motion to a minimum.
The platform swung without a stabilization system engaged, and the platform's deflection was estimated visually with a ruler, so the measurement results aren't especially precise.

At a sampling rate of 500Hz in the ±2g range, the precision accelerometer does a good job converting an acceleration amplitude of 0.0002g or 2mm/s/s.

The next pair of graphs lets you compare the signal-to-noise ratio depending on the sampling rate at a platform deflection of about 1mm.
The sampling rate for the left graph is 125Hz and 500Hz for the right one:
     
A sampling rate of 125Hz corresponds to a low-pass filter cutoff frequency of 31.25Hz for the precision accelerometer. Such a low-pass filter suppresses 50Hz, which is needed for seismic measurements at industrial sites, where, for example, ventilation equipment can generate strong noise at 50Hz and its harmonics.

Lowering the upper boundary frequency of the range by a factor of N reduces the noise by a factor of √N,

The sampling rate of the precision accelerometer is 4 times the upper boundary frequency, 
so reducing the sampling rate from 500Hz to 125Hz will reduce the noise level by a factor of 2, which is confirmed by the last pair of graphs.
This dependence of noise on frequency lets you estimate the minimum measurable acceleration at different sampling rates.
Example:
The acceleration of the swinging calibrator varies at a frequency of 0.64Hz; to get a satisfactory time resolution the sampling rate can be reduced by a factor of 16 to 31.25Hz. In that case the noise will decrease by a factor of 4, and one period of the signal will be represented by 31.25 / 0.64 =  49 samples.
This means  at a sampling rate of 31.25Hz in the ±2g range, the precision accelerometer will be able to convert an acceleration amplitude of 0.00005g or 0.5mm/s/s with the same amplitude resolution as an acceleration of 0.0002g at a sampling rate of 500Hz.

Note: don't confuse the accelerometer's signal-estimation resolution described above with the resolution of the ADC it uses:
the 20-bit ADC of the precision accelerometer in the ±2g range provides a resolution of (2g - ( -2g)) / 2^20 = 0.0000038g,
but because of noise, which depends on the upper boundary frequency of the range, only a substantially stronger signal can actually be distinguished. 

Noise measurement.

Accelerometer documentation often lists the root-mean-square noise level at a sampling rate of 100Hz.
For the 16g accelerometer, the cutoff frequency of the internal low-pass filter at this rate is half as low, 50Hz.
For the 8g accelerometer, the nearest cutoff frequency, 62.5Hz, is determined by a sampling rate of 250Hz.
So the accelerometer noise was measured at sampling rates of 100Hz for the 16g accelerometer and 250Hz for the 8g accelerometer.
