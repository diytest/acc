accelerometers with analog outputs

Three-axis accelerometers with analog outputs.

Accelerometers with digital interfaces USB, RS-485; accelerometers with analog outputs, ranges from ±2g to ±500g, low-frequency and high-frequency (up to 30kHz), with software for measuring and displaying results, turnkey kits.
Three-axis accelerometers with analog outputs are fitted with amplifiers, allowing them to work over a long cable, with a load resistance of 200 Ohm.
To digitize the accelerometer signals and feed the digitized data into a computer, you can use
Synchronous ADC 12-bit, 4+4 channels, USB, 0.5MB memory
or
a modified version of the 8-channel thermocouple controller (8-channel 12-bit ADC, up to 240000 samples of memory, USB interface, USB power) - see below.
Two 3-axis accelerometers with analog outputs can be connected to such devices.



 A board can be made for a dust- and splash-proof enclosure, as shown in the picture,
 or a rectangular board with mounting holes at the corners
 See dimensions on the page Accelerometer with RS-485 interface



In accelerometers built on the MMA7260QT MEMS accelerometer chip, the measurement range (±1,5g/ ±2g/ ±4g/ ±6g),is selected by soldering a pair of jumpers on the board.
In Analog Devices' MEMS accelerometer chips with analog outputs, there is no way to switch ranges. To select the needed range, you need to purchase and install on the board the corresponding chip variant. Their advantage compared to the MMA7260QT is a lower level of intrinsic noise, parameters rated over temperature, and higher thermal stability.
To the 8-channel ADC, you can connect two 3-channel accelerometers

High-frequency accelerometers with analog outputs

An accelerometer built on high-frequency MEMS accelerometer chips provides the following parameters:
measured acceleration range    ±70g
frequency range                                0...30kHz.

The MEMS accelerometer chips of this type are single-axis.
One or two MEMS accelerometers can be installed on the board (giving the accelerometer one or two measurement axes respectively).

MEMS accelerometers with ranges of ±250g and ±500g can also be installed; the frequency range is also 0...30kHz.

When working with an ADC connected to the computer's USB, power for the accelerometers is also supplied from the computer's USB, passing through the ADC board.

Accelerometers in enclosures, cables, and a single-channel ADC with 512KB of memory are shown in the picture. The ADC dimensions are 65x65mm.
Free wires on the cable are for external triggering of the measurement from a button or another device.



If two accelerometers are installed on the board for two-axis measurement, it can be connected to the 8-channel ADC

Strike: simultaneous measurement with two accelerometers of different bandwidth.

Two boards with different accelerometers were glued together to record a strike; the measurement axes of both accelerometers were oriented in the same direction.
One board with a high-frequency accelerometer, ±70g, 0..30000Hz, the other board - with an accelerometer, ±35g, 0..400Hz,
The sampling rate of the signals from both accelerometers was 80kHz (one sample every 12.5µs).

        

Note: the strike was light, so that clipping would not occur.
The black graph - the signal recorded by the high-frequency accelerometer (maximum amplitude 97g); the red one - by the accelerometer with a frequency band of 0..400Hz (maximum recorded amplitude 28g).
In other words, the response of accelerometers with different frequency bands to a strike differs greatly.
The same pair of accelerometers responds identically to slow processes (rocking or vibration at frequencies up to a hundred hertz).

Low-pass filters are used to normalize the frequency response of accelerometers (see Accelerometer for impact measurement ) .
The upper cutoff frequency of the low-pass filter should be much lower than the upper frequency of the accelerometer.
If in the measurement described above, low-pass filters with an upper cutoff frequency of 200Hz and a steep rolloff are applied after both accelerometers, the results will be practically identical.
For impact testing, it is recommended to use low-pass filters with standardized frequency responses and specific upper cutoff frequencies, for example, 1000Hz, 200Hz. It is precisely the upper cutoff frequencies of the filters that are recorded in impact test reports, and using filters with identical characteristics allows comparison of test results obtained on different test equipment.

Conclusion: when choosing an accelerometer, the primary factors to consider are the range of measured accelerations and the frequency range.

If you measure fast processes with a low-frequency accelerometer, the graph will lack detail over time.
Unfortunately, accelerometers with a wide frequency range that are also able to measure small accelerations (with a low noise level) are not manufactured,
the reason: the higher the frequency, the lighter the mechanics must be, and the lighter it is (the smaller the dimensions of the sensing elements), the worse the sensitivity.

Chips for high-frequency MEMS accelerometers with ±70g, ±250g or ±500g, 0..30kHz are expensive and scarce;
whereas if you need to measure accelerations of 20g...500g, but at a lower frequency (up to 400Hz), the corresponding MEMS accelerometer chips are about ten times cheaper.

When measuring small accelerations (for example, less than 2g) with a ±70g range accelerometer, the useful signal can only be seen after processing (smoothing); otherwise it will be lost in the accelerometer's noise.
In terms of time resolution, smoothing is equivalent to lowering the sampling rate, and accordingly, lowering the time resolution.

Note on choosing an ADC:  for accelerometers with analog outputs, the noise level is mainly determined by the MEMS accelerometer chips,
other circuit components and blocks, including the cable and the ADC, contribute only a small amount of noise.
So the noise level of the unsmoothed signal will be the same, regardless of the ADC sampling rate chosen by the operator;
this means that a signal recorded at a sampling rate of 700kHz can be smoothed so that the signal's time resolution is equivalent to the resolution obtained when recording at a sampling rate of 70kHz, while the noise level is significantly reduced through smoothing.
In other words, the noise level of the smoothed signal when recording at 700kHz will be much lower than that of a signal recorded at a sampling rate of 70kHz with the same time resolution.
The price for this improvement in signal quality is a reduction in recording time:
for a single-channel signal at a sampling rate of 700kHz, the memory is only enough to record for 0.3s,
at a sampling rate of 70kHz, the recording time will be 3s.

8-channel ADC, 12-bit, memory for 240000 samples, USB interface, powered from USB or autonomous

View of the ADC board - based on the 8-channel thermocouple controller , jumpers are installed instead of the thermocouple signal-amplifier chips.
Board dimensions 70 x 65mm
Data is recorded into memory on the board, then transferred to the computer over USB
Recording can be started either by a computer command or by closing a pair of contacts on the board - in the latter case an autonomous power source can be used: select the operating mode under computer control, disconnect the controller from the computer's USB, and start the measurement with the button.
Once the measurement is complete, connect the computer and read the data.
The number of samples is set by the user (30000, 60000, 90000, 120000, 150000, 180000, 210000 or 240000 samples can be specified)
The sampling rate is chosen by the user:
-minimum sampling rate 225 Hz
-maximum frequency
  up to 185 kHz with one channel selected
  up to 34 kHz with 8 channels

In the photo, two 2-axis accelerometers are connected to the board (each containing two ADXL001-70g MEMS accelerometers). The accelerometers are powered through the board from USB.

It is recommended to use sampling rates equal to 2 to the power of N, for example,  1024 Hz (or 2^10), 2048 Hz (2^11), ..., 32768 Hz (2^15) - this will give a spectrum with a step of a whole number of Hz.
The software allows you to
-display signal graphs
-compensate the initial signal level (for example, the acceleration of free fall, 9.8m/s/s, for the Z axis)
-select fragments of interest on the signal graphs
-save the selected fragment as a txt or csv file (a csv file opens in MS Excel)
-obtain a spectrum graph for the selected fragment
-determine the decay for the selected fragment (calculating and plotting a linear trend using the least squares method, as in MS Excel)
