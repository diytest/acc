Impact Testing of Equipment

The system can be used, for example, as a measuring instrument in the test rig shown in the video of bicycle helmet impact tests . A dummy of a human head is used as the inertial mass, and the helmet under test is placed on the dummy; the sensor of the impact meter - the accelerometer - is installed inside the dummy head.



ISO 6487:2002 sets out a number of requirements for the measurement and recording system used in impact testing. 

"The following equipment shall be available when conducting the test:
a) an accelerometer with a minimum amplitude range of 200g,
a lower frequency limit of no more than 1Hz,
an upper frequency limit of at least 3000Hz.

b) an analog-to-digital data acquisition system capable of recording shock disturbances as an "acceleration - time" graph, at a minimum sampling rate of 1000Hz.
The data acquisition system must include an analog low-pass filter to suppress interference, with a cutoff frequency of at least 1000Hz and at most 20% of the sampling rate, and a minimum rolloff of 40dB/octave".



Among the commercially available MEMS accelerometers, high-frequency single-axis accelerometers are suitable for this task with a measured acceleration range of ±250g in a frequency range of 0...22000Hz.
Low-frequency MEMS-accelerometers are significantly cheaper, but their frequency range is limited, at best, to 1600Hz (whereas at least 3000Hz is required)..



The requirement to use an analog low-pass filter (LPF) with a rolloff of at least 40dB/octave.
On the internet you can find literature justifying the need to use specifically analog low-pass filters for this task - not digital filters implemented on a DSP or on switched-capacitor chips, and even less so filters implemented in software on a computer.

It is known that a Chebyshev filter provides the steepest frequency-response rolloff, but it also introduces significant phase distortion, which would distort the measurement results.
Acceptable in terms of phase characteristics is a Butterworth filter, which is also mentioned in ISO 6487:2002 .
An 8th-order analog Butterworth low-pass filter can provide a frequency-response rolloff of no worse than 40dB per octave.

The impact measurement system includes

A board with a single-axis accelerometer

measured acceleration range ±70g or ±250g, or ±500g (chosen by the customer) and a frequency range of 0...22000Hz

 
the board with the accelerometer and the cable soldered onto it lies on top of the housing of the data processing/recording unit.
The accelerometer board also carries a voltage regulator and a buffer amplifier that lets the signal be transmitted over a cable several meters long to a low-impedance load in the data processing/recording unit.

Data processing/recording unit.

The data processing/recording unit contains a precision analog filter and an analog-to-digital converter board with memory.
Synchronization of the recording with the impact is done automatically; the user can set in software, the time interval before the impact that will be shown on the graph (for more details, see the help file for the software StrikeTestHelp).

Analog filter

Using a low-pass filter with a normalized frequency response makes it possible to obtain identical results when measuring with accelerometers of different frequency characteristics (provided that the upper frequency of the accelerometers is much higher than the low-pass filter's upper cutoff frequency).
The active analog 8th-order Butterworth low-pass filter is built using high-quality operational amplifiers, with precision capacitors and resistors, which provides temperature and time stability of the filter's frequency response (FR).
The data processed by the filter is fed to the ADC input.

Frequency response of the filter used in the measuring instrument.

Analog-to-digital converter (ADC)

Digitization of the analog signal coming from the low-pass filter is performed by the 12-bit ADC board, with 512KB of memory for the recorded data,
The ADC connects with a cable to the USB port of a computer (laptop, netbook).

The ADC's operating mode (sampling rate, measurement time, measurement start) is selected by the operator in the computer application.
The converted data is recorded into a memory chip on the ADC board, and once the measurement is complete, this data is transferred from memory over the USB cable to the computer, where it is saved to files and displayed as graphs.

System power

is supplied from the computer's USB port. No additional power sources or cables are required.
