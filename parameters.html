Accelerometer parameters

Samples and the ADC

The accelerometer converts the acceleration acting on it into an electrical signal, which is conditioned (amplified or attenuated, and, if needed, a constant offset is added to it) so that its possible levels correspond to the input range of the analog-to-digital converter (ADC).

The ADC periodically samples the signal, i.e., it stores the current value of the signal and converts it into a digital code proportional to that value. 

Depending on the number of bits in the ADC, the code takes positive integer values in the range from 0 to 2N-1, where N is the number of bits in the ADC
So, for a 10-bit ADC the code can take values from 0 to 1023, and for a 12-bit ADC - from 0 to 4095.
ADC code values are dimensionless; they are also called ADC units

Samples are taken at equal time intervals T;
Fsample (the sampling rate) is the value inverse to the interval between samples: Fsample = 1 / T

In the following picture, the blue graph represents the ADC's conditioned input signal, and the samples are represented by red dots

The samples arrive at the computer sequentially, roughly in the form: 519 535 530 ... (these are samples #1, 2, 3...)

Zooming in, we get the following picture:

The picture shows that the signal values and the samples differ: a sample in ADC units is the nearest whole number corresponding to the value of the sampled signal.

The computer reconstructs the signal from the samples; in the simplest case the samples on the graph are connected by straight lines, and the degree of graph distortion is smaller the greater the number of ADC bits and the higher the sampling rate (the higher these parameters, the more expensive the device):

To recover the real time values, it is enough to divide each sample number by the sampling rate.
To display accelerations on a graph, you need to subtract the constant component from the sample values - for the graph shown, this is 512 ADC units - and multiply the resulting value by the conversion coefficient from ADC units to acceleration.
The conversion coefficients are determined by calibrating each accelerometer and are stored in the file Scales.scl, in the same folder as the application. The file is a text file and can be opened in Notepad.

Notes:
-frequency band vs. sampling rate:
here the frequency band refers to an accelerometer with an analog output; the sampling rate is determined by the ADC connected to the accelerometer, and to convert a signal at a frequency of 22kHz the ADC sampling rate must be at least twice as high;
-the maximum sampling rate for the digital accelerometers offered on this site is 3.2kHz (3200Hz), with a frequency band of 0..1600Hz (the upper frequency being half the sampling rate).

In the first picture, with a pulse duration of 2ms, only the accelerometer with a 22kHz band measures the signal accurately enough; the accelerometer with a sampling rate of 3200Hz gives a heavily distorted graph, and the accelerometer with a sampling rate of 400Hz practically "doesn't notice" such a fast change in acceleration.

An impact of 8ms duration is already reproduced reasonably well at a sampling rate of 3200Hz (second picture).
And only a "slow" pulse with a duration of 32ms, with gentle decay and rise, is reproduced practically identically by both a high-frequency accelerometer with a 0..22kHz band and low-frequency accelerometers with sampling rates of 3200Hz and 400Hz.

The listed sampling rates and frequency bands are typical of commercially available MEMS accelerometers (accelerometers in chip-scale packages):
22kHz - accelerometers with analog output exist that are able to convert mechanical impact in the frequency range 0...22000Hz
(more precisely, the frequency response is flat up to 10kHz, with a rise (resonance) at 22kHz of 7dB, and a rolloff of -3dB at 32kHz);
these accelerometers are designed for recording impact loads; they have low sensitivity; accelerometers are available with ranges of ±70g, ±250g and ±500g
the price of just the chip for such an accelerometer is a few tens of euros

3200Hz - the maximum sampling rate for the digital accelerometers offered on this site (USB, RS-485)

400Hz - there is a series of accelerometers with various ranges and a built-in low-pass filter with a frequency band of 0...400Hz.

Measurement range and acceleration amplitude

In the following three pictures, the acceleration (blue graph) is displayed by the samples (red dots) for different ratios between the range of the input signal and the accelerometer's measurement range.
In simple terms, the blue graph is what we are measuring, what is acting on the accelerometer, and the red graph is what we will see on the computer monitor.

in the first picture, the selected accelerometer range ±2g matches the amplitude of the input signal, and the signal is reproduced from the samples with maximum resolution


in the second picture the amplitude of the input signal, 100g, significantly exceeds the selected accelerometer range of ±16g; when the signal level goes outside the accelerometer's measurement range, clipping occurs


the third picture shows how the signal is displayed when its amplitude, 0.02g, is much smaller than the selected accelerometer range of ±2g.
As described above, the ADC transmits integer codes, i.e., between samples #46 and #47 the difference is one ADC unit, and the corresponding acceleration of 0.002g is obtained by multiplying the ADC units by the conversion coefficient.
Acceleration changes that are too small relative to the accelerometer's range will be displayed as steps 



Conclusions:
when choosing an accelerometer for correctly recording an acceleration, it is a good idea to know the acceleration parameters - for example, for an impact, the measured acceleration range and the duration of the impact; such parameters can be found in specialized literature.
On the internet you can find the parameters of impacts in sports, or the acceleration parameters for objects falling onto different surfaces.

If devices for ensuring human safety are being tested, you can be guided by the fact that a pilot loses consciousness at loads of about 10g, but this load is relatively sustained - lasting about seconds; an untrained person can also withstand the same load for, say, a dozen milliseconds. For ordinary conditions, an acceleration two to three times lower is considered safe.

When testing industrial structures, what is usually of interest is not the transmission of the impact, but the structure's response to the impact: the resonant frequency, the decay of oscillations. More about this here

G-force

g is used in astronautics, aviation, motorsport, and more broadly in engineering as a unit for measuring g-force - the increase in body weight caused by its accelerated motion. The permissible value of g-force for civil aircraft is 4.33 g. An ordinary person can withstand g-forces up to 5 g.
Trained pilots in anti-g suits can withstand g-forces up to 9 g. Resistance to negative, upward-directed g-forces is significantly lower. Usually at −2 to −3 g vision "reddens" and a person has a harder time tolerating such a load due to blood rushing to the head.

There is some minor terminological confusion on this topic: for example, the definition of g-force above gives a person standing still a g-force of 0g, but in the table below this same case is treated as a g-force of 1g. A similar issue occurs when measuring pressure: we say - pressure 0, meaning the pressure of one atmosphere around us, while a scientist would say - pressure 0, meaning the complete absence of molecules in a given volume.

Approximate g-force values encountered in everyday life
A person standing still 1 g
A passenger in an airplane during takeoff 1,5 g
A parachutist landing at a speed of 6 m/s 1,8 g
A parachutist when the parachute opens (with a speed change from 60 to 5 m/s) 5,0 g
Cosmonauts during descent in a Soyuz spacecraft up to 3.0—4.0 g
A pilot performing aerobatic maneuvers up to 5 g
A pilot pulling an aircraft out of a dive 8,0—9 g
A (sustained) g-force corresponding to the limit of human physiological capability 8,0—10,0 g
The greatest (short-term) g-force in a car crash that a person is known to have survived 214 g
